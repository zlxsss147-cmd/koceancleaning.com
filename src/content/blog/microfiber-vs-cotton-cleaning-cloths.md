If you only remember one sentence: **microfiber wins on performance and cost-per-use, cotton wins on price and compostability — the right choice depends on the task, not the label.** Microfiber absorbs roughly seven times its weight, leaves no lint, and survives 500+ washes. Cotton is cheaper to buy, biodegradable, and perfectly good for spills, polishing and food service where a little lint doesn't matter.

We make and export both types, so this is a spec sheet, not a sales pitch. Below is the head-to-head buyers actually ask for when they are filling a container.

## Microfiber vs cotton at a glance

| Attribute | Microfiber | Cotton |
|---|---|---|
| **Material** | 70–80% polyester + 20–30% polyamide; split-fiber synthetics | Natural cellulose cotton |
| **Absorption** | Holds ~7× its weight; lifts dirt via capillary action + static | Holds ~its weight; slower wicking |
| **Lint** | Lint-free — safe on glass, screens, auto paint | Sheds lint, especially when new |
| **Durability** | 500+ washes (no softener, low heat) | ~50–100 heavy washes; pills over time |
| **Cost per use** | Higher buy price, lower cost per use | Low buy price, higher cost per use at volume |
| **Sustainability** | Petroleum-based; sheds microplastics; recycled-PET options exist | Renewable, biodegradable; water-intensive farming |
| **Best for** | Glass, electronics, auto, healthcare, dust | Polishing, spills, food service, compostable needs |

## What microfiber actually is

Microfiber is a synthetic fabric woven from filaments finer than a strand of silk — typically 0.1–0.3 denier. Each filament is "split" during finishing so the fiber has tiny hooked edges. That split structure is the whole trick: the hooks create surface area that traps dirt and moisture through **capillary action**, while the polyamide content builds a small static charge that pulls in dust.

![Diagram of a split microfiber filament trapping a dust particle — the split edges and capillary action that make microfiber clean without chemicals.](/images/blog/microfiber-vs-cotton/microfiber-anatomy.png)

A typical cleaning cloth blends **80% polyester (structure) with 20% polyamide (absorbency)**, or 70/30 for a stiffer, scrubbier feel. Weight is measured in **GSM (grams per square meter)**: 200–300 GSM for general wiping, 300–350 GSM for glass and heavy-duty, 350+ GSM for buffing. ## What a cotton cleaning cloth is

Cotton cloth is woven or knitted natural cellulose. It is soft, highly absorbent in absolute terms, and — importantly for many buyers — **renewable and compostable**. A cotton terry or waffle weave is the default in kitchens, food service and any setting where a cloth is used once and discarded. The trade-offs are lint (especially on the first washes), faster wear, and no static-assisted dust pickup.

## Head-to-head: the five things buyers argue about

**Absorption and water use.** Microfiber pulls more liquid, faster, and wrings out almost dry — which is why it dominates window and surface cleaning where streaks are the enemy. Cotton soaks up a spill just fine; it just stays wetter and leaves more behind.

**Lint and scratches.** This is where microfiber separates from cotton. On glass, lenses, screens and freshly painted auto panels, cotton leaves fibers behind; microfiber leaves nothing. The one caveat: microfiber only stays scratch-free if you rinse trapped grit out of it first. A dirty microfiber is grittier than a clean cotton rag.

**Durability.** A well-cared microfiber cloth runs 500+ wash cycles. Cotton pills, thins and loses shape after roughly 50–100 heavy washes. At a janitorial scale, that gap is the difference between reordering quarterly and reordering yearly.

**Cost per use.** Cotton wins the sticker price; microfiber wins the math. One microfiber replaces many cotton rags over its life, so the effective cost per clean is usually a fraction — the reason high-volume buyers standardize on microfiber for repeat tasks.

**Sustainability and hygiene.** Cotton is the greener story on paper: it grows, it rots, no microplastics. But it is thirsty to farm, and a damp cotton rag is a bacterial reservoir if not laundered hot. Microfiber is petroleum-based and sheds microplastics in the wash, yet its longevity cuts replacement waste. Recycled-PET (rPET) microfiber and [OEKO-TEX® MADE IN GREEN](/blog/what-is-oeko-tex/) certified lines close the gap for buyers who need a defensible sustainability claim.

## Which should you source?

- **Standardize on microfiber** for glass, electronics, automotive, healthcare and dusting — anywhere lint, streak-free finish and wash-life matter.
- **Keep cotton in the mix** for food service, polishing, one-use spills and markets where compostability is a selling point.
- **Mixed programs win.** Most distributors we supply run 70–80% microfiber for repeat tasks and 20–30% cotton for the rest. You get the cost-per-use of microfiber without giving up cotton's compostability story.

## How to specify microfiber when you source

If you are buying microfiber, these are the lines every quote should state:

- **GSM** — 200–300 general, 300–350 glass/heavy, 350+ buffing.
- **Blend** — 80/20 (more absorbent) or 70/30 (scrubbier); confirm it is a true split-weave, not a printed imitation.
- **Edge** — laser-cut or ultrasonic-sealed edges shed less lint than stitched borders.
- **Color coding** — assign colors by zone (restroom, kitchen, floor) to prevent cross-contamination; this is a selling point for janitorial buyers.
- **Certification** — ask for a currently valid [OEKO-TEX® STANDARD 100](/blog/what-is-oeko-tex/) number if the cloth touches skin or enters the EU/UK retail chain.

## Three myths worth dropping

- **"Microfiber scratches everything."** Only dirty microfiber does. Clean, quality microfiber is used on car paint and camera lenses.
- **"Cotton is always greener."** Renewable, yes; low-impact, not automatically — farming water and per-use replacement both count.
- **"They're interchangeable."** They are not. Specifying the wrong one means lint on a showroom window or a rag that falls apart after a month.

## FAQ

**Q: Is microfiber better than cotton for cleaning?**
A: For most professional cleaning, yes — microfiber absorbs more, leaves no lint, and survives 500+ washes. Cotton is cheaper upfront, biodegradable, and better for tasks where lint and reusability don't matter. Choose by task, not by habit.

**Q: How many times can you wash a microfiber cloth?**
A: Quality microfiber lasts 500+ washes if you skip fabric softener and avoid high-heat drying. Cotton wears and pills sooner, typically 50–100 heavy-use washes before it loses shape.

**Q: Do microfiber cloths scratch surfaces?**
A: Clean, good-quality microfiber is non-abrasive and safe on paint, glass and screens. The real risk is grit trapped in the fibers — always rinse and inspect before using on a delicate finish.

**Q: Are cotton cloths more environmentally friendly?**
A: Cotton is renewable and biodegradable but water-intensive to grow. Microfiber is petroleum-based and sheds microplastics, yet lasts far longer, lowering per-use impact. Recycled-PET and OEKO-TEX® MADE IN GREEN options narrow the gap.

**Q: Which is cheaper per use?**
A: Cotton is cheaper to buy; microfiber is cheaper per use because one cloth replaces many and survives hundreds of washes. At container volumes, microfiber usually wins on total cost.

**Q: What GSM microfiber cloth should I source?**
A: 200–300 GSM for general wiping, 300–350 GSM for glass and heavy-duty, 350+ GSM for buffing. A 70/30 or 80/20 polyester/polyamide blend grabs more; 80/20 holds slightly more water.

## The practical verdict

There is no universal winner — only the right cloth for the job. Build your program around microfiber for repeat, lint-sensitive, high-volume tasks, keep cotton for compostable and polishing use, and spec both by GSM, blend and edge so you are buying performance, not a label.
