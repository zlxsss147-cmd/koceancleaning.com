If you only remember one sentence: **a cellulose sponge is an open-cell foam made from regenerated plant cellulose — most Swedish-style dishcloths are a 70% cellulose / 30% cotton blend — cheap, very absorbent and biodegradable in principle, but short-lived and quick to stiffen when dry.** This guide shows what it is made of, where it beats the alternatives, and what to check when you source one — so you buy the right grade instead of the cheapest one.

## What a cellulose sponge actually is

A cellulose sponge is a foam whose solid structure is regenerated cellulose — cellulose dissolved from wood pulp and then cast and set into a sponge with a network of open pores. Those pores are what let it suck up and hold water.

The best-known form is the **Swedish dishcloth**: a flat, semi-rigid sheet typically made of **70% cellulose and 30% cotton**. The cellulose supplies the absorbency; the cotton gives the cloth wet strength so it does not fall apart when saturated.

![Material composition of a Swedish-style cellulose dishcloth — 30% cotton for wet strength, 70% cellulose from plant cell walls for absorbency, from Kocean Cleaning.](/images/blog/cellulose-sponge/cellulose-material-blend.jpg)

Two things follow from that plant-based origin:

- **High absorbency.** The open-cell structure lets a quality cloth hold roughly up to 20 times its own weight in liquid.
- **Biodegradability in principle.** Cellulose and cotton are plant-derived and can be broken down by microbes — unlike polyester microfiber or PVA foam. How fast depends on additives, dyes and local conditions.

## Absorbency in practice

The absorbency claim is not just marketing. A cellulose dishcloth can hold a glass's worth of water in the sheet, and wring most of it back out — enough to mop up a spill with one pass.

![Ultra-absorbent cellulose dishcloth under running water, with a glass demonstration showing the cloth holding a large volume of liquid — from Kocean Cleaning.](/images/blog/cellulose-sponge/cellulose-ultra-absorbent.jpg)

For buyers, the practical takeaway: cellulose cloths are excellent where you need to **pick up a lot of water cheaply** — counters, sinks, spills, dishwashing. They are not the right tool where you need a streak-free, lint-free finish; there a [microfiber cloth](/blog/how-to-choose-microfiber-cleaning-cloths/) is better.

## Biodegradability and environmental claims

Sustainability is the main reason many buyers switch to cellulose. A cellulose/cotton cloth is plant-based and, under the right conditions, compostable.

![A cellulose dishcloth in soil beside a young seedling, illustrating the 100% compostable and biodegradable positioning of plant-based cleaning cloths, from Kocean Cleaning.](/images/blog/cellulose-sponge/cellulose-compostable.jpg)

A caution worth stating plainly: "biodegradable" depends on the whole product, not just the fiber. Synthetic scrim, glues, dyes and antibacterial coatings can slow or prevent breakdown. If compostability matters to your market, ask the supplier for a recognized certification rather than relying on the word "natural" — the same documentation discipline we recommend for [OEKO-TEX®](/blog/what-is-oeko-tex/) claims.

## Where a cellulose sponge works best

- **Kitchen and dishwashing** — cheap, absorbent, gentle on most dishes.
- **Spill cleanup** — holds a large volume of liquid in one pass.
- **Bathroom and general household wiping** — soft, disposable, low cost.
- **Light scrubbing** — use an abrasive-backed grade for stuck-on food.

![A green cellulose dishcloth being squeezed out in a hand with soap foam, showing its soft, flexible wet texture during dishwashing, from Kocean Cleaning.](/images/blog/cellulose-sponge/cellulose-sponge-in-use.jpg)

For anything lint-sensitive — glass, mirrors, screens or finished paint — reach for microfiber instead. Cellulose can shed fine fibers as it wears.

## Cellulose vs microfiber vs PVA

The three most common wipe materials solve different problems. Here is how they stack up:

| Attribute | Cellulose sponge | Microfiber | PVA cloth |
|---|---|---|---|
| Absorbency | Up to ~20× own weight | Moderate, controlled | Very high |
| Biodegradable | Yes, in principle | No (synthetic) | No (synthetic polymer) |
| Wash durability | Low (weeks–months) | High (200–500 washes) | Medium |
| Scrubbing bite | Medium (higher with abrasive side) | Low | Very low |
| Dries stiff | Yes | No | Yes |
| Best at | Cheap spill pickup | Lint-free repeat use | Streak-free drying |

If your buyers care about sustainability claims, note that only cellulose is plant-based — but verify with a real certification. If they care about streak-free car drying, a [PVA cloth](/blog/what-is-pva-microfiber-cloth/) or a [twisted loop drying towel](/blog/twisted-loop-microfiber-drying-towels-1000-gsm/) is the better tool.

## How to source the right grade

A short checklist before you place an order:

- **Blend ratio** — 70% cellulose / 30% cotton is the common Swedish-dishcloth spec; confirm what you are actually getting.
- **Intended use** — general wiping, dishwashing or scrubbing? Pick plain, scrim or abrasive-backed accordingly.
- **Size and thickness** — confirm the finished sheet dimensions and whether the cloth ships dry and compressed.
- **Absorbency spec** — ask for a measured figure (e.g. × own weight), not a vague "super absorbent" claim.
- **Certifications** — food-contact, compostability and antibacterial claims should be backed by documents.
- **Packaging** — cellulose cloths are usually shipped dry and compressed; confirm expansion and count per pack.
- **Supplier check** — samples, spec verification and a factory review, as in our [7-point manufacturer checklist](/blog/how-to-vet-cleaning-products-manufacturer/).

## Care and lifespan

Cellulose cloths are short-lived by nature, but you can get more from them:

- Rinse thoroughly after each use to remove food and grease.
- Wring out excess water and let them dry between uses — a damp, closed cloth breeds odor.
- Do not boil or expose to high heat.
- Replace when they start to break apart or smell, even after washing.

If your goal is a reusable, lint-free tool rather than a low-cost disposable, compare with [microfiber vs cotton](/blog/microfiber-vs-cotton-cleaning-cloths/) before you commit.

## FAQ

**Q: What is a cellulose sponge made of?**
A: A cellulose sponge is made from regenerated cellulose — usually dissolved wood pulp — cast into an open-cell foam and set. A very common Swedish-style dishcloth blend is 70% cellulose and 30% cotton: the cellulose brings high absorbency and the cotton adds wet strength. Some grades add a scrim for durability or an abrasive layer for scrubbing.

**Q: Is a cellulose sponge biodegradable?**
A: In principle yes — the base material is plant-derived cellulose (and often cotton), which microbes can break down. Plain, undyed cellulose cloths compost far more readily than ones with synthetic scrim, glue or coatings. Treat "biodegradable" as a claim to verify with a certification, not a given — how fast it breaks down depends on additives, dyes and local conditions.

**Q: How absorbent is a cellulose sponge compared to microfiber?**
A: Swedish-style cellulose cloths are commonly rated to absorb up to about 20 times their own weight in liquid, thanks to their open-cell structure — excellent for spills and wet wiping. Microfiber holds less water per gram but releases it more controllably, is lint-free and survives hundreds of washes. Cellulose wins on cheap, high-volume absorption; microfiber wins on durability and streak-free results.

**Q: Why does a cellulose sponge get hard and stiff when dry?**
A: Because water is what keeps the cellulose foam soft and pliable. As it dries, the pores collapse and the material stiffens into a board-like state. Soak it in water for a few minutes and it softens right back up. This is normal for cellulose (and for PVA) — store it damp if you want it ready to grab.

**Q: What is a cellulose sponge best used for?**
A: General kitchen and household wiping, soaking up spills, washing dishes, bathroom cleaning, and light scrubbing (especially grades with an abrasive side). It is a low-cost, reusable-to-disposable tool. For glass, screens or any lint-sensitive surface, use a microfiber cloth instead.

**Q: How long does a cellulose sponge last?**
A: A kitchen cellulose cloth typically lasts a few weeks to a couple of months in regular home use — far fewer wash cycles than microfiber, which can reach 200–500. Cellulose/cotton blends (70/30) hold up better than pure cellulose because cotton adds wet strength. Rinsing well and drying between uses extends its life.
