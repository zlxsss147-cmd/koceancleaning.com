Nonwoven fabrics power a huge share of the cleaning products you buy — but the difference between them matters a lot more than most buyers realize. Choose the wrong fabric and your product will shed, pill, soak up too little, or fail to pass a customer's quality test.

This guide compares the four nonwoven technologies Kocean works with every day, and gives you a simple decision rule for each one.

## What "nonwoven" actually means

A nonwoven is a fabric made directly from fibers — without the weaving or knitting step. Fibers are laid into a web and bonded together by mechanical, chemical or thermal means. That's why nonwovens are fast to produce, cheap per meter, and endlessly tunable.

The four technologies differ in **how the fibers are bonded**, and that determines everything: strength, softness, absorbency, cost, and where the fabric belongs in your product line.

## Spunlace (hydroentangled) — the soft, absorbent all-rounder

Fibers are entangled by high-pressure water jets. No binders, no heat — just mechanical locking.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Feel** | Soft, cloth-like, drapes well |
| **Strength** | Good, but lower than spunbond wet strength |
| **Absorbency** | Excellent |
| **Best for** | Wet wipes, cleaning cloths, face towels, medical disposables |

**Decision rule:** if your product touches a surface to clean it — wipes, cloths, pads — spunlace is your default answer. It's the fabric your customers will describe as "feels like cloth."

## Spunbond — the strong, economical workhorse

Polymer granules are melted, extruded into filaments, and bonded by heat and pressure. This makes the strongest, cheapest nonwoven.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Feel** | Firm, papery, some rustle |
| **Strength** | Highest — great wet strength |
| **Absorbency** | Low |
| **Best for** | Bags, covers, geotextiles, disposable aprons, packaging |

**Decision rule:** if the product needs to *hold together* more than *absorb* — bags, sleeves, covers — spunbond wins on cost and strength.

## Meltblown — the fine filter

Meltblown uses hot air to blow melted polymer into ultra-fine fibers (a few microns). The result is a dense, micro-porous web.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Feel** | Soft, linty, fragile |
| **Strength** | Low — usually needs a support layer |
| **Absorbency** | Exceptional at filtration |
| **Best for** | Filter media, masks, oil-absorbent pads, barrier layers |

**Decision rule:** if the job is *filtering or blocking particles*, meltblown is the only choice. Almost always used as a core layer inside a stronger composite.

## Needle-punched — the durable, industrial felt

Barbed needles mechanically punch and entangle the fibers into a dense felt. Thick, tough, and highly durable.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Feel** | Dense felt, thick, abrasive-tolerant |
| **Strength** | Very high, dimensional stability |
| **Absorbency** | Good, holds liquid in structure |
| **Best for** | Industrial wipers, floor pads, automotive, abrasive backings |

**Decision rule:** if the product faces heavy friction or needs to stay thick and stable — floor pads, industrial wipes, polishing discs — needle-punched felt is the workhorse.

## How to decide in three questions

1. **Does it clean a surface?** → spunlace (or needle-punched for heavy industrial).
2. **Does it hold or protect something?** → spunbond.
3. **Does it filter or block particles?** → meltblown (usually as a composite layer).

## The honest advice for buyers

Most real products are **composites** — a meltblown core sandwiched between spunbond layers for a respirator, or a spunlace top with a spunbond backing for a heavy-duty wipe. Don't force a single-fabric answer.
