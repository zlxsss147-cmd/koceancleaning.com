Nonwoven fabrics power a huge share of the cleaning products you buy — but the difference between them matters a lot more than most buyers realize. Choose the wrong fabric and your product will shed lint, pill after two wipes, absorb too little, or fail a customer's quality test at the dock.

This guide compares the four nonwoven technologies Kocean runs on its own lines every day, with the **actual numbers buyers ask about**: weight in grams per square meter (GSM), fiber composition, and the process that decides the fabric's character. Read it once and you'll be able to write a spec sheet a factory can actually quote.

## What "nonwoven" actually means

A nonwoven is a sheet of fabric made **directly from loose fibers** — without spinning yarn and without weaving or knitting. Fibers are laid into a web and then bonded by one (or a combination) of three mechanisms:

- **Mechanical** — fibers are entangled by water jets (spunlace) or barbed needles (needle-punch).
- **Thermal** — a polymer web is fused by heat and pressure (spunbond, meltblown).
- **Chemical** — a binder resin glues the fibers (less common in cleaning grades, used in some waddings).

That's why nonwovens are fast to produce, cheap per meter, and endlessly tunable: change the fiber, the weight, or the bond, and you get a different fabric. The four technologies below differ mainly in **how the fibers are bonded**, and that single choice determines strength, softness, absorbency, cost, and where the fabric belongs in your product line.

> Quick mental model: **spunlace = soft cloth, spunbond = strong film-like sheet, meltblown = filter web, needle-punched = thick felt.**

## Spunlace (hydroentangled) — the soft, absorbent all-rounder

A carded web of staple fibers is passed under **high-pressure water jets (typically 40–250 bar)** that entangle the fibers together. No binder, no heat, no adhesive — just mechanical locking. The result feels like cloth because, structurally, it almost is.

| Spec | Typical range |
|---|---|
| **Basis weight** | 30–120 gsm (wipes 35–60, cloths 50–90, industrial 90–120) |
| **Fiber** | Viscose (rayon) / polyester (PET) blends — 50/50, 70/30, or 100% viscose; also 100% PET, cotton, or PP |
| **Fiber fineness** | 1.2–1.6 dtex (viscose), 1.5–3.0 dtex (PET) |
| **Jet pressure** | 40–250 bar, multiple entangling manifolds |
| **Absorbency** | Excellent — viscose holds roughly its own weight in water; blends feel dry-to-the-touch yet wipe wet |
| **Best for** | Wet wipes, dry cleaning cloths, facial/makeup pads, medical disposables, food-service wipes |

**Decision rule:** if your product *touches a surface to clean it* — wipes, cloths, pads — spunlace is your default. It's the fabric your customers will describe as "feels like cloth." For a disposable wipe, start at **35–50 gsm, 50/50 viscose/PET**; for a reusable-style cleaning cloth, move to **60–90 gsm**.

## Spunbond — the strong, economical workhorse

Polymer granules (almost always **PP**, sometimes PET) are melted, extruded through a spinneret into continuous filaments, drawn, and bonded by **heat and calender pressure** into a uniform sheet. This makes the strongest, cheapest nonwoven per square meter.

| Spec | Typical range |
|---|---|
| **Basis weight** | 10–150 gsm (commonly 15–80 for converted goods) |
| **Polymer** | PP (hydrophobic) or PET; PP is 20–40% cheaper |
| **Filament size** | 1.5–3.0 denier (fine), up to 6 denier for stiff grades |
| **Line speed** | Very high — PP lines can exceed 500 m/min |
| **Absorbency** | Low (PP repels water) — a hydrophilic finish can be added if needed |
| **Best for** | Bags, covers, sleeves, disposable aprons, diaper backsheet, geotextile, protective packaging |

**Decision rule:** if the product needs to **hold together more than absorb** — bags, sleeves, covers, dust bags — spunbond wins on cost and strength. Note PP is hydrophobic: if you need it to wick, ask for a **hydrophilic treatment** (it adds a small cost but changes behavior completely).

## Meltblown — the fine filter

Meltblown extrudes molten polymer through a **die with very fine capillaries** while **hot air (250–400 °C)** blows the strands into ultra-fine fibers — typically **0.5–5 microns** in diameter. The result is a dense, micro-porous web that traps particles by interception and, when electrically charged, by electrostatic attraction.

| Spec | Typical range |
|---|---|
| **Basis weight** | 10–100 gsm (filter media usually 15–60) |
| **Fiber diameter** | 0.5–5 µm (sub-micron common) |
| **Polymer** | PP (MFI 300–1200), occasionally PET or PA |
| **Capture range** | ~0.1–10 µm particles; the core layer in N95/FFP2 respirators |
| **Strength** | Low — almost always needs a support layer |
| **Best for** | Filter media, masks, oil-absorbent pads, barrier layers, wipes for fine dust |

**Decision rule:** if the job is **filtering or blocking particles**, meltblown is the only choice. It's almost never used alone — it sits as a core layer inside a stronger composite (spunbond–meltblown–spunbond, the "SMS" structure you'll see on medical gowns). For an oil-absorbent pad, meltblown PP is ideal because it's both oleophilic and hydrophobic.

## Needle-punched — the durable, industrial felt

Barbed needles (**hundreds to thousands per board, punching at 500–4000 strokes/cm²**) mechanically interlock a thick web of staple fibers into a dense felt. Thick, tough, dimensionally stable, and happy under friction.

| Spec | Typical range |
|---|---|
| **Basis weight** | 80–2000 gsm (floor pads 300–800, industrial wipers 150–400) |
| **Fiber** | PET, PP, viscose, jute, even glass or aramid for extreme duty |
| **Needle density** | 500–4000 punches/cm² |
| **Finish options** | Calendering, impregnation, coating, or laminating for extra stiffness |
| **Absorbency** | Good — holds liquid within its structure |
| **Best for** | Industrial wipers, floor pads, automotive interiors, polishing discs, geotextile, abrasive backings |

**Decision rule:** if the product faces **heavy friction or needs to stay thick and stable** — floor pads, industrial wipes, polishing discs, machine covers — needle-punched felt is the workhorse. It's also the most recycling-friendly option, since it readily uses **recycled PET (rPET)** staple.

## Which nonwoven belongs in *your* cleaning product?

Mapping the fabric to a concrete product line makes the choice obvious:

- **Wet wipes / facial wipes** → spunlace, **35–50 gsm, 50/50 viscose/PET** (soft, high wet strength, low lint).
- **Reusable-style cleaning cloth** → spunlace, **60–90 gsm**, or a spunlace/spunbond laminate for scrubbing power on one side.
- **Heavy-duty industrial wiper** → needle-punched **200–400 gsm PET**, often with a scrubbery texture.
- **Floor cleaning pad / dust mop refill** → needle-punched felt with a calendered or abrasive face.
- **Dust bag / protective cover / apron** → PP spunbond, **20–50 gsm**, hydrophilic if it must wick.
- **Filter pad or respirator layer** → meltblown core (PP, **20–40 gsm**) inside an SMS or spunbond-supported composite.
- **Oil-spill / kitchen oil pad** → meltblown PP — oleophilic and lint-free.

## A spec sheet you can actually send to a factory

Most misquotes happen because the brief is vague ("a good wiping cloth, around 60 grams"). Here is the minimum a supplier needs to quote accurately — copy it and fill in your target:

```
Product:            [e.g. all-purpose cleaning wipe]
Technology:         [spunlace / spunbond / meltblown / needle-punched / composite]
Target basis weight: [__] gsm  (tolerance ±[__]%)
Fiber composition:  [e.g. 70% viscose / 30% PET]
Color:              [white / custom Pantone __]
Width / roll size:  [__] cm × [__] m
Finish:             [plain / embossed pattern __ / hydrophilic / print __]
Absorbency target:  [__] g water per g fabric (optional)
Lint requirement:   [Helmke level __ / none visible after 5 wipes]
Packaging:          [folded, labeled, PE bag of __ pcs]
Monthly volume:     [__] rolls / [__] pcs

```

Send that to a manufacturer who runs **multiple** lines, and you'll get a real quote plus a sample — not a guess. (This is the exact brief Kocean's team works from every day.)

## FAQ

**Q: Can a spunlace wipe be biodegradable?**
A: Yes — a **100% viscose (or cotton) spunlace** is compostable. Blends with PET are not, though they can be made partially bio-based with PLA. If "flushable" or "compostable" is a claim you need, say so up front; it changes the fiber choice.

**Q: Why does my PP spunbond bag repel water?**
A: PP is naturally hydrophobic. If you need it to absorb or wick, specify a **hydrophilic finish** — it's a standard, low-cost treatment.

**Q: Which is cheapest?**
A: **Spunbond (PP)** per square meter. But compare by *function*: a 40 gsm spunlace wipe and a 40 gsm spunbond sheet are not interchangeable, so "cheapest" only matters within one application.

**Q: Can I combine technologies?**
A: Almost always yes, and you should. Common composites: **SMS** (spunbond–meltblown–spunbond) for filtration/barrier, **spunlace + spunbond** for a soft-top / scrubby-back wipe, **needle-punch + scrim** for a stable floor pad. Composites are where most real products live.

## The honest advice for buyers

Don't pick a fabric from a spec sheet alone. Most finished products are **composites** — a meltblown core inside spunbond for a respirator, or a spunlace top laminated to a spunbond backing for a heavy-duty wipe. Force a single-fabric answer and you'll either overpay or underperform.
